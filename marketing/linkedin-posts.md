## Post 2 — The DG pre-check pain point

**Goal:** demonstrate real expertise, attract junior operators specifically.

> The question that should come before every DG shipment booking:
>
> "Has anyone actually confirmed this is Non-DG, or are we assuming?"
>
> I've seen bookings get pulled at the last minute because nobody asked for the UN number, proper shipping name, class and packing group until the carrier asked first.
>
> A 5-step pre-check before you book:
> 1. Confirm DG classification with the shipper directly — don't assume "Non-DG" without it in writing
> 2. Get the MSDS/SDS, even if it "shouldn't be needed"
> 3. Confirm the carrier actually accepts that DG class
> 4. Check packaging and labeling requirements
> 5. If anything is uncertain, escalate to a DG specialist before booking — not after

> This isn't a replacement for formal DG training. It's the operational habit that stops you from finding out the hard way.
>

## Post 3 — "Shipment will miss vessel" workflow

**Goal:** shareable, practical value — the kind of post other operators screenshot.

> Cargo is running late and the vessel leaves in a few hours. Here's the order I actually work through it in — not panic, just steps:
>
> 1. Confirm the *actual* vessel ETD and gate/document cut-off (not the one you remember from the booking)
> 2. Confirm exactly where the cargo is right now and how long it needs to reach the terminal
> 3. Check if a later cut-off or a sister vessel on the same string exists
> 4. If not recoverable — identify the next vessel or an air/courier alternative
> 5. Get customer approval on cost/timing before rebooking anything
> 6. Update everyone (warehouse, trucker, agent, customer) once the new plan is confirmed
>
> Nothing revolutionary here. But when you're under pressure, having the order memorized is what keeps you from missing step 2 because you jumped straight to step 4.
>
> #FreightForwarding #VesselLogistics #Shipping #SupplyChainManagement

## Post 4 — AI prompt in action (novel, differentiated content)

**Goal:** show the AI-prompt angle that most competitors don't offer — this is your differentiation on display.

> Started using AI as a second pair of eyes for shipment risk — not to replace judgment, to catch what I might miss when I'm juggling five shipments at once.
>
> The prompt I use most:
>
> "You are assessing the risk of a shipment not meeting its deadline. Deadline: [date/time]. Current status: [where cargo/docs stand]. Known issues: [delays, missing docs, DG concerns]. Time remaining: [hours/days].
>
> Identify the top 3 risks to this shipment meeting its deadline, rate each low/medium/high, and suggest one mitigation action for each."
>
> Paste your real shipment details in and it gives you a structured second opinion in seconds — genuinely useful before a stressful handover call.

## Post 5 — The "nobody trains you on this" post

**Goal:** relatable pain point for trainees/junior operators, builds trust before any pitch.

> Nobody hands you a manual when you start in marine logistics.
>
> You learn the pickup request email by copying an old one and hoping the format still works. You learn the DG pre-check by getting a booking rejected once. You learn how to handle a missed vessel by watching someone more senior do it under pressure, once, and trying to remember it three months later.
>
> That trial-and-error is normal — but it doesn't have to be the only way. Structured checklists and templates don't replace experience, they just mean you're not rebuilding the same wheel every single operator before you already built.
>
> If you're a few months into this role and still figuring out your own system: what's the one thing you wish someone had just handed you on day one? Genuinely curious — might turn the best answers into the next checklist I build.
>
